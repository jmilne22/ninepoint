Ninepoint — early-game experience review, 6 September 2026

**Verdict: preserve the city, the cast, and the walk–talk–play loop. Restructure the teaching between Pip and the first full game, and give the school transition a stronger personal reason.** The opening is welcoming and the progression is unusually forgiving. Its largest weakness is that successful tutorial clicks do not establish readiness to choose moves, finish a game, or judge the count. The fiction then moves on as though that understanding has been secured.

My predicted newcomer response is: “I like these people and this place. I can capture stones now. But I am not sure why this game is over, whether this score is right, or what I am trying to accomplish at school beyond following instructions.” That is an interpretation from an AI walkthrough, not a quotation from a human participant.

I completed New Game through Noor and Ivo, including full counted games. I deliberately used simple captures, short connections, replies to obvious threats, and enclosed walls resembling the lessons. I did not use autoplay, a move-selection bot, or engine recommendations to choose my moves. Existing player saves were preserved through a separate user-data directory. No game or public API changes were made.

**1. Writing**

**The small, physical details are the strongest writing.** Wren moves cups so the player can sit down. Dov is missing a stone and offers a game while the washing runs. The quay bench has dry middle slats. These establish people doing ordinary things before they become opponents. A newcomer can understand the social invitation without knowing Go. Preserve this economy and use it more often in the school scenes. [Dov’s introduction](before/163_laundry_npc_hello.png), [quay bench](before/133_quay_bench_2.png).

**The opening supplies a destination more strongly than an ambition.** Hana welcomes the player and says that people play Go throughout Verhaven. The attic note points to Pip; Pip points to Wren; Wren points to Kesh; Kesh points to Hana. These directions are generally understandable. The previous tenant’s board creates a little curiosity, but that curiosity has little consequence within this slice. At the Instituut, “Your five fixtures prepare you for the Cup” introduces a goal without much reason to care about it. Likely reaction: “I understand where to go, but why does my character want this?” Give one novice or Kesh a personal reason to care about the upcoming competition, and let the player encounter that reason before registration. One ordinary conversation would do more than additional lore or an inventory quest. [Registration and Cup direction](before/199_registered_next_line.png).

**Wren’s experience question ignores the immediately preceding game.** After Pip, the choices include “Never. I don’t know the rules,” “I know how the stones work,” and a request to be reminded about capturing. I selected the complete beginner route to inspect the teaching, but none precisely expresses the experience the game just gave me. A newcomer may wonder whether Capture Go counted or whether they chose the wrong answer. Add an explicit “Pip showed me Capture Go. What comes next?” route that acknowledges the successful first lesson and explains what full Go adds. On a later visit, Wren also sent me to get a novice card I already held; make that referral acknowledge the card. These are small continuity fixes. [Experience choices](before/38_wren_experience_choices.png), [stale referral](before/141_tomas_first.png).

**The novices have memorable possessions but limited conversational identities.** Noor protects a postcard from tea; Ivo arrives by bicycle with a pencil; Lea offers scrap paper; Sora offers a cushion. These are good introductions. They then converge on “Shall we play?” and similar menus. Ivo repeated his introductory situation when I returned after declining. During the games, Noor and Ivo shared lines such as “Those are yours,” “A ko,” and “I’ll take another look.” The default voice fallback corroborates that similarity. Likely reaction: “I can remember their props, but I haven’t learned what distinguishes playing or talking with them.” Give each a different immediate concern about the novice group and a distinct response to one or two actual board events. This needs a few specific lines, not an affection system or lengthy dialogue trees. [Noor introduction](before/23_noor_meeting.png), [Ivo introduction](before/19_ivo_greeting.png).

**The first fixture endings need a better conversational landing.** After losing, Noor said, “You won. I’d like another try after I’ve looked at that,” then immediately offered another game. Ivo said, “Your game. Thanks for sitting down with me,” and also returned to the game offer. These correctly acknowledge the result, but barely acknowledge a first league encounter or what happened on the board. The likely effect is a transaction ending where a relationship between fellow players could begin. Let Noor name something she wants to examine, and let either player recognize that this was one fixture completed. Offer a rematch after that beat, with wording consistent with whether they want a break. [Noor reaction](before/57_noor_post_match.png), [immediate offer](before/58_noor_after_win_2.png), [Ivo reaction](before/117_ivo_post_match.png).

Kesh’s return after the deliberately resigned practice did offer a useful new possibility: a position about getting a group out of trouble. Keep that connection between playing and an optional lesson. The immediate loss response itself was simply “That’s a win for me. Thanks for playing.” Her documented rival role is not yet strongly felt in the reviewed opening; her current card explanation is more distinctive as administration than as rivalry.

**2. Progression**

**Highest priority: teach how a game ends before asking the player to finish one.** The first full-game reminder says, “Pass with P when you have finished playing,” then asks the player to check marked dead groups. It explains the controls but assumes the judgment those controls require. On this route, Two Eyes came at school, after Wren’s full game. I sought out Tomás’s counting lesson, but only after already completing that first count. Likely reaction: “How do I know I’m finished? Why is a stone still on the board dead? Am I allowed to accept this?” This affects confidence in the result, not just tactical strength. [First-game instructions](before/81_practice_rules.png).

At Wren’s count, changing the mark on White J3 changed Black’s displayed total from 15 to 11. The interface allowed the edit, but did not explain why the original mark was correct or how the point change was composed. The score breakdown appeared after acceptance. Before the full practice game, walk through a small nearly finished position: a useful remaining move, a safe group, an unmistakably dead group, two passes, and the resulting score. Then repeat that explanation on the player’s actual first ending. Keep the dead-group editing control, but give a beginner a reason to trust or question each mark. [Count after toggling J3](before/123_wren_count_toggle.png), [accepted result](before/124_wren_result.png).

**There are many instructed moves and too few decisions.** Taking Wren’s complete rules route plus her optional opening plan produced 13 single-move exercises: three liberties, three capture, two self-capture, and five opening exercises. Their answers are usually prescribed. This can produce success without teaching what to notice independently. “Choosing a First Move” also spends much of its sequence completing already constructed enclosures. I imitated those visible walls during the games, including unnecessary first-line moves. That is a plausible transfer from the examples, although it does not prove every newcomer would do the same. Use fewer compulsory examples, then ask the player to choose between two meaningful alternatives and show an opponent reply. Keep enclosure geometry, but explicitly distinguish an opening position from a finished territory boundary. [Opening enclosure task](before/69_opening_3_task.png).

One small wording error reinforces this confusion: the liberties exercise says to “Join the two black stones” even though those two stones already touch. “Extend this group” would describe that position accurately. [Position](before/44_liberties_group.png).

**Wren’s live practice prompts can ask for something that is absent.** “Support or connect a loose stone nearby” appeared when my two Black stones were already joined. “Notice a group with only one liberty” appeared when no group had only one liberty. Source inspection after play confirms that these prompts advance at fixed move thresholds rather than checking the position. A newcomer may search fruitlessly or conclude that they do not understand liberties. Make the prompt conditional on a suitable position and highlight the relevant group on request. If it is merely a future lookout, say so. Wren’s promise to help if stuck should also lead to a visible help action. [Connection prompt](before/84_wren_4.png), [liberty prompt](before/87_wren_10.png), implementation at the reviewed base commit (`src/go_ui/go_match.gd:330`).

**The repeated passing exposed the same missing judgment.** Saved games contain seven player passes against Wren, five against Noor, and seven against Ivo. The opponent continued placing stones after the earlier passes. All three games eventually reached counting. An opponent continuing is legitimate; this is not evidence that the opponent should automatically agree to end. But the player gets little help understanding whether those moves still matter or what response is necessary. Explain one actual late-game continuation in the first practice, and make clear when the player can keep passing safely. Separately assess whether the novice opponents prolong settled positions with low-value moves. Do not change their behavior solely to make this walkthrough shorter. [Ivo’s first pass](before/107_ivo_pass1.png), [eventual count](before/113_ivo_pass7.png).

**The school repeats the easy part while delaying the needed part.** Hana’s entry problem asks for another basic capture after Pip, Wren’s capture lesson, and a full game. It could function as a reassuring check, but currently feels like restarting the curriculum. The route then crosses back to registration, reads the league board, returns to class, and finally meets novices. After solving Hana’s problem, the objective changed before her follow-up conversation; I had to speak to her again for the contextual response. Replace the repeated capture with a question arising from the practice game, or a short welcome that introduces a peer. Consolidate the registration and class directions so that this transition feels like joining a group. [Hana problem](before/185_hana_capture_problem.png), [post-problem world](before/187_hana_after_puzzle.png).

**The honest rank and loss handling are worth preserving.** Kesh explicitly says 30 kyu is a starting entry, not a measured placement. “There’s no game to pass here” removes an unnecessary anxiety. I could decline practice, go to school, and later return. Losing Wren’s game did not hold up that path. Two rated wins moved the rank from 30k to 28k; resigning Kesh’s unrated practice left it at 28k. The league showed the actual completed fixtures and next opponent. This is a clear, humane progression foundation. Make the ordinary HUD as informative as the league board: “2/5 fixtures — next: Lea” would be more useful than continuing to direct me to the novice room while I am inside it. [Kesh invitation](before/136_kesh_invitation_2.png), [league after two fixtures](before/119_league_after_two.png).

The novice games gave me obvious captures and were winnable with deliberately elementary play. Wren’s result was close. Those observations do not validate the displayed ranks, establish human difficulty, or resolve PROG-01 / CONTENT-05. Independent beginners still need to play without my pre-existing Go knowledge or documentation exposure.

**3. Setting**

**Verhaven already has an appealing identity in play.** Brick, tram rails, puddles, storefronts, washing machines, the kettle sign, and the water give useful landmarks and a coherent everyday setting. The laundrette particularly succeeds because playing while waiting is an understandable human activity. The quiet quay offers a welcome change of pace. Preserve these places and environmental lines; they create curiosity without adding a parallel progression system.

**De Ketel and the Instituut look different more strongly than they behave differently.** The club has warm brown surfaces, cups, a counter, coats, and close tables. The school has colder surfaces, formal rooms, a registrar, and a league board. That distinction is visible. The documented opposition between rank as a document and rank as something demonstrated at a table is much less evident in the opening’s conversations: the club issues the card and everyone cooperates in sending the player onward. A newcomer can understand “club versus school,” but has little reason to feel caught between two Go cultures. Show one concrete difference in what someone values about the same game or result. Kesh and Hana need not argue dramatically; a brief difference in emphasis would make the setting’s central idea playable. [Club](before/145_club_free.png), [school arrival](before/178_institute_arrival_actual.png).

**The novice room undersells the people described in its dialogue.** Five similar tables sit in a large plain room. The postcard, scrap paper, and spare cushion that make the introductions specific are not visibly distinguishing the tables. Sora mentions low seats, but the displayed arrangement does not clearly show them. Likely reaction: “These are five opponent stations.” Add a few readable personal objects and arrange the space as a shared room people actually use. This would strengthen existing writing without requiring more text. [Novice room](before/07_novice_room_arrival.png).

**The core directions mostly worked; the review destination needs a landmark.** Pip’s park, the kettle sign, the west-end tram stop, and the lower west novice-room door all gave usable reasons to move. I navigated using the displayed world and directional inputs. There were some alignment corrections at doors and furniture, but the harness makes these unsuitable evidence for human movement difficulty. The review notification, however, named the quay without telling a first visitor how to find it; the HUD continued to describe school fixtures. I eventually found the southern route from the park. Add “south past the park” when first offering the quay, or allow tracking that destination. Keep the discovery and quiet location. [Quay review board](before/134_ivo_quay_review.png).

**4. Other: presentation, controls, and review**

**Lesson feedback hides the evidence it is explaining.** After a correct move, a large central card covers the group or boundary. Advancing often replaces the position with the next exercise. This happened in liberties, capture, opening, counting, and Two Eyes lessons. A beginner needs to inspect the changed board while reading why the move worked. Put the explanation beside the board, highlight the changed stones/liberties, and leave that state available until the player advances. This is a learning fix as much as a layout fix. [Covered liberties result](before/45_group_answer.png), [covered counting result](before/151_count_1_result.png).

**Old legality warnings remain after legal moves.** Against Ivo, an illegal J7 attempt correctly produced “That would be self-capture.” After legal H6 and an opponent reply, the same warning remained. It was still present after I later legally played J7 and captured stones. This is directly observed, separate from the harness interruptions. A newcomer could interpret it as criticism of a move the board accepted. Clear transient errors after a successful action, or visually label them as an earlier event. [Illegal attempt](before/98_ivo_ko_attempt.png), [legal H6 with stale warning](before/99_ivo_66.png), [legal J7 capture with stale warning](before/100_ivo_68_capture.png).

**The world objective needs a reliable background.** Its dark text is difficult to read over floors, fences, foliage, and water, and the longer directions wrap across the lower screen. This also makes rank and objective text visually collide with the environment. Use a restrained opaque strip or consistently contrasting panel, with shorter location-aware objectives. The board itself and the main dialogue cards were substantially easier to read. [Club objective](before/145_club_free.png), [novice-room objective](before/07_novice_room_arrival.png).

**The review is valuable, but its advice assumes judgment the lessons have not established.** I requested Noor’s review, inspected its four cards, and later requested Ivo’s, left while it was processing, and retrieved it at the quay. That background completion and retrieval worked. The board diagrams and marked alternatives are worth keeping. However, Noor’s review repeatedly told me to leave the first line alone until the end after the opening exercises had taught me to finish walls. Ivo’s review said, “Connect when a cut would hurt; when it would not, take the bigger point.” A newcomer’s unresolved question is precisely how to tell whether the cut hurts. Explain one concrete consequence or provide a short replayable comparison. Qualify the first-line advice so that necessary saves and captures are not apparently forbidden. [Noor advice](before/55_noor_review_mistake1.png), [Ivo connection advice](before/137_ivo_review_mistake1.png).

The Ivo D7 explanation also named the already connected C7/E7 stones while omitting the isolated D8 stone that motivated my connection. This does not establish that the engine’s preferred move or point estimate was wrong. It shows why a generic explanation can miss the learner’s actual question. Later “leaned on the white stone” adds unexplained Go language. Prefer plain descriptions tied to the displayed position over an authoritative-sounding proverb.

**Review timing weakens the emotional response to a game.** The sequence is result, review question, optional processing/cards, and then the opponent’s world dialogue. Reading Noor’s full review delayed her reaction to our first fixture. Give the immediate win/loss response first, then let that person offer analysis. Preserve the ability to leave processing and read the review later. The summary can also clarify that “best moves” means comparison with engine choices, not a beginner pass mark: Ivo’s larger win yielded only three best moves and two other moves that gave nothing away out of 40 placements. Without context, a novice could read a successful first win as a poor grade. [Noor summary](before/53_noor_review_progress.png).

Smaller presentation fixes: keep a review recommendation together instead of placing only “take the bigger point” on another page; expand the league’s P/W/L labels or provide help; explain that its Entry column is the frozen starting rank. Preserve the disclosure that other results are simulated, but place the technical explanation in appropriate table help. The resignation confirmation correctly allowed cancellation; in an unrated practice it could explicitly reassure the player that rank stays unchanged. [Five-word review page](before/138_ivo_review_mistake2.png), [resignation prompt](before/153_kesh_resign_prompt.png).

**Recommended order of work**

| Priority | Change | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Teach finishing/counting before the first full game; make Wren’s help match the position. | The player must understand the result and know what action is being requested. |
| 1 | Keep lesson boards visible; clear stale board warnings. | Current presentation can actively undermine correct understanding. |
| 2 | Reduce prescribed exercises and replace the repeated school capture with a relevant next step. | Build independent judgment and improve the transition’s pace. |
| 2 | Give school entry and the first two fixtures personal meaning; improve immediate post-game reactions. | Supply a reason to continue beyond completing instructions. |
| 2 | Make review advice concrete and place the human reaction before analysis. | Turn an engine comparison into something the learner can use. |
| 3 | Fix continuity lines, novice voice repetition, HUD contrast, quay directions, and room details. | Strengthen an already promising world and remove recurring friction. |

Small writing fixes can proceed independently: acknowledge Pip in Wren’s question; use “extend” in the existing liberties position; recognize an already issued card; avoid Noor requesting immediate play after saying she wants time to inspect the game; give novices individual event lines; introduce unfamiliar terms at their point of use. Moving lessons, changing the first-game support, and consolidating school entry are curriculum/story-order decisions and should be treated separately.

**Recommended revised early-game sequence**

1. Keep the short Hana welcome, attic board, and note to Pip. Add one small glimpse of something the player might eventually want to join or achieve.
2. Keep Pip’s Capture Go as the first action and immediate success. Let his referral explicitly promise learning what happens beyond capturing one stone.
3. Have Wren acknowledge Pip. Use a small number of rules exercises, including a genuine choice and an opponent reply. Offer extra repetition separately.
4. Use Wren or Tomás to demonstrate a nearly finished small game: territory and prisoners, one clear living/dead comparison, a final useful move, passing, and the count. Keep this on the board rather than teaching rules in dialogue.
5. Play Wren’s unrated 9×9 with an accessible help action and a guided finish. React to the actual result immediately. Offer one useful review position afterward.
6. Keep Kesh’s provisional card before any optional handicap practice. Give her invitation a personal connection to the novices or Cup.
7. Take the tram. Let Hana welcome the player into a group and identify a useful next concept from their practice. Combine the administrative directions and avoid making another basic capture the central arrival event.
8. Meet Noor as a fellow beginner before the first fixture. After the game, show its human consequence and the updated table; then point toward Ivo. After Ivo, make the next opponent and the emerging Cup goal visible.

This preserves the documented inspirations: the accessible walk–talk–play competition loop of Pokémon TCG and Tag Force, the patience and personal learning associated with Hikaru no Go, and the deliberate presentation ceremony documented for nigiri’s HammerLock influence. Those are design references read before play, not evidence that a newcomer understands the world. The recommendation does not require bringing back M37’s calendar, affection, inventory errands, or competing progression systems.
